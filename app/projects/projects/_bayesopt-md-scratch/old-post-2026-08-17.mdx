summary: 'Coarse-grained force fields need parameters, and fitting them is slow expert work. I built a benchmark where the answer is known and ran Bayesian optimisation against an LLM. The LLM won, and not for the reason I expected.'

Coarse-grained molecular dynamics is how you simulate a biopolymer film rather than a
handful of molecules. You trade atoms for beads, buy three orders of magnitude in system
size and simulated time, and pay for it with a parameterisation problem: every new
molecule needs numbers that nobody has fitted yet. For the bio-based materials space —
glycerol, alginate, chitosan, the plasticisers and biopolymers that actually go into
films — that fitting is slow, manual, expert work, and it is the bottleneck.

Each candidate parameter set costs a simulation to evaluate, so this is a
sample-efficiency problem, which is the natural home of Bayesian optimisation. The
question I wanted to answer is whether a frontier LLM, with no surrogate model at all,
is competitive with that.

## The result

| method | best loss | crash rate | mean parameter error |
|---|---|---|---|
| Random search | 0.9264 | 63% | 43.8% |
| Bayesian optimisation | 0.5613 | 39% | 47.2% |
| **LLM** | **0.1247** | **0%** | **7.24%** |

The LLM is **4.5× better on loss and 6.5× better on parameter recovery**, and it is the
only method that crosses "good enough" — it does so at simulation nine and stays there.

## Why it wins, and it is not what I assumed

My first assumption was that the LLM was simply a better optimiser. The data says
something narrower and more interesting.

**It never crashes the simulator.** Zero failures in 90 simulations, against 39% for
Bayesian optimisation and 63% for random search. Stability constraints in molecular
dynamics are analytic — the timestep limit follows from the bond vibration period, the
cutoff limit from the box geometry — and the LLM simply knows them. A Gaussian Process
has to discover that boundary by crashing into it, and each crash buys nothing. In a
space where half the volume returns no number, knowing where not to look is worth more
than modelling where to go.

That capability is robust. It replicated on a completely different system (argon, whose
stability boundary is governed by unrelated physics), and it survives when the targets
are withheld entirely.

**The surrogate has nothing to learn from at this budget.** I checked whether the GPs
were predicting anything at all, by fitting each observable on pooled data and scoring on
held-out points:

| observable | R² at 20 training points | at 100 |
|---|---|---|
| potential energy | 0.60 | 0.71 |
| mean bond | 0.62 | 0.84 |
| bond spread | 0.41 | 0.77 |
| mean angle | 0.31 | 0.79 |
| angle spread | **−0.26** | 0.37 |
| pressure | **0.04** | 0.16 |
| diffusion | **−0.11** | 0.13 |

Twenty points is what the surrogate has after warm-up. At that size three of the seven
models are worse than predicting a constant. There is nothing to improve architecturally
when there is not enough data to fit anything — which is why every BO variant I tried,
tuned or untuned, single- or multi-output, landed in the same place.

So the honest claim is not "LLMs beat Bayesian optimisation". It is that **below roughly
fifty simulations in nine dimensions, a surrogate has nothing to work with, and priors
are the only information available.** The same table predicts BO should become
competitive somewhere around 60–100 simulations. That prediction is untested.

## What the LLM gets wrong

It reads observables too literally. The measured mean bond length is 0.2996 nm, and the
model proposed **exactly 0.2996** as the equilibrium bond length in two of three runs.
The true value is 0.3126 — a bond in a dense liquid sits about 4% below its equilibrium
length, because neighbouring molecules compress it. Equating the ensemble average with
the parameter is a rookie error, and no amount of further optimisation corrected it.

Withholding the numeric targets confirms this is copying rather than inference: the
values scatter to 0.2850, 0.2900 and 0.2600, and the loss degrades from 0.1247 to 0.3352
— though the crash rate stays at exactly zero. Roughly half the accuracy advantage was
privileged data. The feasibility advantage was not.

The standard fix in coarse-grained parameterisation is to fit bonded parameters to full
*distributions* from an atomistic reference rather than to their means, which
structurally cannot make this mistake. This result is an argument for doing that.

## What this does and does not show

It shows the optimiser, not the physics. Tuning a model to reproduce itself says nothing
about whether a three-bead Lennard-Jones glycerol is a good glycerol — it is not, since
without partial charges it has no hydrogen bonding and its cohesive energy comes out
around half the experimental value.

The scope is narrow and worth stating plainly: **one molecule, one budget, one model,
one mapping.** The LLM arm ran three seeds against ten for the others, so its margin is
a strong signal rather than a settled number. And the setting — nine parameters fitted
simultaneously against one composite objective — is close to the worst case for Bayesian
optimisation. Real workflows stage the problem, fitting bonded parameters against cheap
atomistic distributions with hundreds of evaluations, then non-bonded parameters against
expensive thermodynamics with far fewer. In that first regime, a surrogate should win.

Which suggests where each method belongs. Cheap objective, many evaluations affordable:
use the surrogate. Expensive objective, few evaluations affordable: use the priors. For
a Martini-style framework that maps onto something concrete — the model selects bead
types from chemistry, and Bayesian optimisation fits the bonded parameters against
atomistic reference data.
